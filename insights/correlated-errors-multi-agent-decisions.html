Correlated Errors in Multi-Agent Decisions

Published 24 October 2026 · Synthetic experiment

Adding more agents does not guarantee more reliable decisions when those agents can fail together. This experiment studies how a shared-shock mechanism affects majority voting and confidence-weighted aggregation.

Key findings

  • Mean majority accuracy declined as the shared-shock mixture probability increased.
  • Across the tested settings, mean accuracy fell from approximately 79.9% at q = 0 to 69.8% at q = 1.
  • Confidence-weighted policies had lower Brier scores than hard majority predictions in this simulation. Calibration adjustment produced only a modest additional improvement.

Experimental design

The simulation used five random seeds, 2,500 scenarios per seed and setting, nominal agent skills of 0.80, 0.70 and 0.62, and q values of 0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75 and 1. Here q is the probability of a shared-shock component in the mixture; it is not a pairwise correlation coefficient.

Accuracy was the same across the weighted policies for each seed and setting. The Brier-score comparison also reflects that majority predictions were evaluated as hard 0/1 outputs, so the metrics should not be interpreted as a like-for-like accuracy comparison.

Limitations

This is a synthetic experiment, not a benchmark of real language models or deployed agent systems. Results depend on the chosen shared-shock mechanism, skills, aggregation rules and calibration assumptions. They do not establish that one policy is universally superior.

Reproducibility

Experiment code and data: correlated-errors experiment repository.

Conclusion

Evaluations of multi-agent systems should test shared failure modes, not just individual agent accuracy. Independence assumptions can materially change how much benefit aggregation appears to provide.
