Finding a Kazakh sentence's twin in Turkish: spelling rules reached 67%, LaBSE 99.8%

OğuzTamga: can a retriever find evidence across five Turkic languages and two scripts, and say so when there is none?

By Muzammal Hussain · 12 November 2026 · Research update

Someone asks a question in Kazakh. The evidence exists, but only in Turkish or Uzbek, and in a different script. I built OğuzTamga to test two things across Turkish, North Azerbaijani, Kazakh, Kyrgyz and Northern Uzbek: whether a retriever can find the matching sentence in another language, and whether an agent can abstain when the matching sentence is not there. The agent never writes an answer; it returns a sentence with its source, or nothing.

Key findings

  1. Spelling rules go a long way: mapping Kazakh and Kyrgyz Cyrillic to Latin and dropping diacritics took a character-level retriever from 17.3% to 67.4% on cross-script pairs.
  2. Sentence embeddings go further: LaBSE found the right sentence 99.8% of the time on every pair type, e5-small 96.8%.
  3. Abstention works when retrieval is strong: with LaBSE the agent made the right move 98.0% of the time and answered only 1.4% of questions that had no evidence.
  4. The benchmark is nearly solved: because FLORES sentences are translations of one English text, these numbers are an upper bound, not a measure of real cross-language search.

Setup

The data is FLORES+, the Open Language Data Initiative's maintained version of FLORES-200: about 1,000 professionally translated sentences per language, aligned across languages. For each of the 20 directed language pairs, every sentence is a query and the sentences of the other language are the candidates. Two of the languages are written in Cyrillic and three in Latin script, so 12 of the 20 pairs cross scripts.

I compared word and character TF-IDF, the same character retriever after a hand-written Cyrillic-to-Latin mapping (plus Uzbek spelling rules) and diacritic folding, and two multilingual sentence encoders, e5-small and LaBSE. In a second test the true sentence was removed for half the queries, chosen with a fixed seed, and the agent had to abstain on those. Its confidence threshold was set on the dev split and applied unchanged to devtest, over five seeds.

One pattern I did not expect: e5-small found Turkish targets hardest, between 87.2% and 94.2%, while every other target language stayed at 95.0% or above. LaBSE showed no such gap. I have not tested why.

A right move is answering with the true sentence when it exists, or abstaining when it does not. The middle two columns are shares of queries whose true sentence was available. Mean of five seeds; the standard deviation across seeds was under one percentage point.

The lexical agent was safe but timid: when the evidence existed it still abstained on about 45% of queries. The confidence signal mattered less than the retriever. The gap between the top two scores worked better than the top score alone for character retrieval, made no difference for LaBSE, and was slightly worse for e5-small.

What this means

For these five languages, an off-the-shelf multilingual encoder already finds translated sentences almost perfectly, and it gives an agent enough confidence to refuse when the evidence is missing. Transliteration is still worth knowing about: where no encoder is available, or as a cheap first filter, a page of spelling rules nearly quadruples cross-script retrieval. The harder and more useful question is the one FLORES cannot answer, because its sentences are translations of each other: can the same agent find evidence in documents written separately in each language? That is the next version.

About the name

Oğuz honours Oğuz Kağan, remembered in Turkic tradition as a common forefather of the Turks. A tamga is the seal a Turkic tribe used as its mark. The agent works the same way: every answer carries the mark of its source. The languages themselves come from three branches of the family: Oghuz, Kipchak and Karluk.

Limitations

FLORES+ sentences share one English source, which favours every method and especially encoders trained on translation pairs. Pools hold about 1,000 sentences; real collections are larger. Missing evidence is simulated by removing the true sentence for exactly half the queries. The transliteration is a simple hand-written mapping, not a standard. No text is generated, so this says nothing about the fluency or faithfulness of written answers.
